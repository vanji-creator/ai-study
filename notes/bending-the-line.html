Block 0 · how a model learns

Bending the line

A model is a curve you are allowed to reshape. Everything about layers and non-linear rules is a question about which shapes are available to you — not about arithmetic.

The goal, with nothing else in it

Three dots. They came from measurement: you put in 1, 2 and 3, and got back 1, 4 and 9. You did not choose these numbers and you cannot change them.

A model is a curve drawn on the same page as the dots. Its parameters are knobs that change the curve's shape. Training is turning the knobs until the curve passes through every dot.

That is the whole job. If a shape that touches all three dots is not available to your curve, no amount of turning helps — and that is the only thing that goes wrong in this page.

One stage · two knobs

Two knobs give you a line, and only a line

The first model has two parameters. Here is what each one actually does to the picture:

weight → tilts the line bias → slides the line up and down

Turn them both. Try to touch all three dots.

You cannot do it, and you will not do it by trying harder. Tilt and height are the only two things you control, and no combination of them puts a bend in a straight line. Press the button above: the best-fitting line misses all three dots at once, and every dot it touches costs you another.

Here is the proof, without any calculation. Get the line to pass through the first two dots exactly — that is possible, and there is only one line that does it. Now look at the third dot. The line arrives at 7 where the measurement says 9. To reach 9 the line would have to be lifted by 2, which drags it off both of the dots it had. One height cannot be two heights.

This is the important distinction: the model is not inaccurate here. The shape it needs is not in the set of shapes it can draw at all.

Two stages · four knobs

Four knobs, and still only a line

The obvious repair is more parameters. Take the output of one weight-and-bias and feed it into a second one — two stages instead of one, four knobs instead of two.

Move all four. Watch the shape, not the numbers.

Press the random button as many times as you like. It is always a straight line.

The readout tells you why: whatever the four knobs are, there is always a single tilt-and-height setting that draws exactly the same line. The second stage simply re-tilts and re-slides what the first stage handed it, and a re-tilted, re-slid straight line is a straight line.

You added knobs but you did not add shapes. That is what people mean when they say a stack of linear layers collapses into one. It is not a statement about algebra — it is a statement about the set of pictures available to you, and that set did not grow.

This is also the answer to the question "do we need another dimension?" The dimension that matters is not on the page. It is how many genuinely different shapes the knobs can reach. Going from two knobs to four moved you from a set of straight lines to the same set of straight lines.

The floor

One rule, and the line can bend

Put a floor between the two stages. The rule is complete in one sentence:

if the value is negative, it becomes zero otherwise, leave it alone

The shaded band below is that floor. The line runs along it — pinned flat, because everything trying to go below zero is held at zero — and then, at one point, it leaves the floor and climbs.

That point is a bend. And where it sits is a knob you did not have before.

Slide the bend left and right. The flat stretch grows and shrinks; the climb starts earlier or later. Neither of those was possible with any of the four knobs in the previous section.

One more thing worth noticing: slide the bend all the way to the left, past the first dot. The line goes straight again. The floor only changes the shape where it is actually holding something back — a layer whose values never go negative is, for that data, still a plain straight line.

Three bends

Enough bends fit anything

One bend is not enough for three dots. So use three, each with its own bend position and its own steepness, added together.

Each slider below controls how steeply one of the three pieces climbs after its bend. Move them and try to hit all three dots. Then press the button.

Exact. Every dot touched.

And notice what nobody had to know. The dots are 1, 4, 9 — they are squares. You could have spotted that and written the curve down by hand. But this fit never used that fact. It used three bends whose positions and steepnesses are ordinary numbers, found the same way the weight and the bias were found: by nudging them downhill.

That is the trade the field made. Rather than guess the right curve for each problem, use many bends and let the data place them. Guessing the curve needs you to already know the answer. Bends need only measurements.

A curve made of enough straight pieces is indistinguishable from a smooth one — the same way a polygon with enough sides is indistinguishable from a circle. Real layers have thousands of these pieces rather than three.
